The Data That Isn't There: A Philosophical Field Guide to Missingness

Nov 02, 2026
13 minute read

An empty cell is not empty

Every grad student has opened a dataset, run summary(), and seen it: the dreaded NA 😱. The usual reaction (and my own past reaction…in the past…I swear) is logistical. How do I get rid of these? Drop the rows, move on to the interesting part.

This post argues that the NA is an interesting part. An empty cell is not a lack of information. It is a record that something happened: a participant didn’t answer, a sensor failed, a patient left the study, a question didn’t apply. The blank has a cause, and that cause is entangled with the very things we’re trying to learn about.

Put philosophically: missing data is a problem about the relationship between the world and our observations of it. Our dataset is not the population. It is the population filtered through a process that decides what we get to see. Every method for handling missingness is, implicitly, a claim about what that filter looks like. And as we’ll see, the most consequential of those claims can never be verified from the data we have.

Three questions to ask before any method

Before reaching for a technique, it helps to separate three different questions that often get blurred together:

  1. Does the value exist? A non-response on an income question hides a real number. But “income at age 40” for someone who died at 35 doesn’t hide anything; there’s no value to recover. “Quality of life after death,” “pregnancy status of male participants,” or “rating of a product never purchased” are not unknown, they are undefined. Treating them as ordinary missing data and imputing them produces answers to questions nobody should be asking.
  2. What is the target? If you want the average outcome among everyone who would complete the study, dropout is part of the definition, not a nuisance. If you want the average outcome had everyone completed, you’re asking a counterfactual question. Missing-data methods can only be judged relative to an estimand.
  3. Why is it missing? This is the question Rubin’s taxonomy addresses, and it’s where the rest of this post lives.

Questions 1 and 2 are conceptual, not statistical. No software will answer them for you, and getting them wrong makes question 3 irrelevant.

History aside

If you have read any of my other posts you’ll know that I can’t help but insert a little history in…sorry. Donald Rubin is an esteemed American statistician born December 22, 1943 in Washington, D.C. USA. His pioneering work has generated important statistical methods and insights including the expectation-maximization algorithm (a methodology to estimate statistical parameters for latent variables now widely used in machine learning), the Rubin causal model (an important method that helped move modern statistics from estimating associations to causation), and most importantly for this the missing data framework and multiple imputation.

Rubin’s missing data framework

In 1976, Donald Rubin gave us the vocabulary we still use. Let \(Y\) be the complete data we wish we had, split into an observed part \(Y_{obs}\) and a missing part \(Y_{mis}\). Let \(R\) be an indicator matrix recording which values we actually see. The key move is to treat \(R\) as a random variable with its own distribution, the missingness mechanism:

\[ P(R \mid Y_{obs}, Y_{mis}, \psi) \]

That one line reframes the whole problem. Missingness isn’t a defect in the data; it is data, generated by a process we can reason about. The three categories describe what that process is allowed to depend on.

MCAR: Missing Completely At Random

\[ P(R \mid Y_{obs}, Y_{mis}) = P(R) \]

Whether a value is missing has nothing to do with anything, observed or not. A lab tech drops a random subset of test tubes. A survey platform randomly shows a question to 70% of respondents. The observed cases are a simple random sample of all cases.

MCAR is the only mechanism under which “just drop the incomplete rows” is generally unbiased. It’s also the rarest in practice, because people and instruments rarely fail at random.

MAR: Missing At Random

\[ P(R \mid Y_{obs}, Y_{mis}) = P(R \mid Y_{obs}) \]

Missingness can depend on things you observed, but not on the missing values themselves once you account for those. Older participants are less likely to complete an online follow-up, and you recorded age. Within any age group, completers and non-completers look alike on the outcome.

The name is famously bad. MAR does not mean missingness is random in any everyday sense; it can be strongly systematic. A better gloss is “random conditional on what we know.” It’s a statement about whether your observed variables are sufficient to explain away the selection.

MNAR: Missing Not At Random

Anything else. Missingness depends on the unobserved values themselves, even after conditioning on everything observed. People with high incomes skip the income question. Patients whose symptoms worsen drop out of the trial. The value is missing because of what it is.

Ignorability

Why does the MAR line matter so much? Because under MAR (plus a technical condition that the parameters of the data model and the missingness model are distinct), the mechanism is ignorable for likelihood-based and Bayesian inference. You can fit a model to the observed data and get valid inference without ever writing down a model for \(R\). Multiple imputation, full-information maximum likelihood, and mixed models all lean on this.

Ignorability is a kind of epistemic permission slip: you may stop worrying about why the data are missing, provided the reasons live entirely in what you observed.

Seeing it in a simulation

Abstract definitions only go so far. Let’s build a world where we know the truth, then hide parts of it in different ways.

set.seed(2026)
n <- 5000

x <- rnorm(n)                      # fully observed covariate
y <- 2 + 1.5 * x + rnorm(n)        # outcome we'll make partly missing

truth <- c(mean_y = mean(y), slope = unname(coef(lm(y ~ x))[2]))
truth
##   mean_y    slope 
## 2.003321 1.482776

Now three missingness mechanisms, each tuned to hide roughly 35–40% of y:

z <- function(v) (v - mean(v)) / sd(v)

r_mcar <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.35)                     # depends on nothing
r_mar  <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.7 + 1.5 * z(x)))  # depends on observed x
r_mnar <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.7 + 1.5 * z(y)))  # depends on y itself

hide <- function(r) ifelse(r == 1, NA, y)

dat <- list(
  MCAR = data.frame(x, y = hide(r_mcar)),
  MAR  = data.frame(x, y = hide(r_mar)),
  MNAR = data.frame(x, y = hide(r_mnar))
)

sapply(dat, function(d) round(mean(is.na(d$y)), 2))
## MCAR  MAR MNAR 
## 0.36 0.39 0.38

Here’s what each mechanism hides. The grey curve is the full distribution of y; the coloured curve is what survives.

plot_df <- do.call(rbind, lapply(names(dat), function(m) {
  rbind(
    data.frame(mechanism = m, which = "All values (truth)", y = y),
    data.frame(mechanism = m, which = "Observed only",
               y = dat[[m]]$y[!is.na(dat[[m]]$y)])
  )
}))
plot_df$mechanism <- factor(plot_df$mechanism, levels = c("MCAR", "MAR", "MNAR"))

ggplot(plot_df, aes(y, colour = which, fill = which)) +
  geom_density(alpha = 0.25) +
  facet_wrap(~ mechanism) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("grey40", "#2C7FB8")) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("grey70", "#2C7FB8")) +
  labs(x = "y", y = NULL, colour = NULL, fill = NULL) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

Under MCAR, the observed data are a faithful miniature of the whole. Under both MAR and MNAR, high values of y are under-represented. Look at the MAR and MNAR panels side by side: from the plot alone, you can’t tell which is which. Hold onto that thought.

Complete-case analysis: the default most of us use

cc <- t(sapply(dat, function(d) {
  d <- na.omit(d)
  c(mean_y = mean(d$y), slope = unname(coef(lm(y ~ x, data = d))[2]))
}))
round(rbind(Truth = truth, cc), 3)
##       mean_y slope
## Truth  2.003 1.483
## MCAR   2.005 1.492
## MAR    1.379 1.472
## MNAR   1.293 1.327

Three lessons, one of which usually surprises people:

  • MCAR: both the mean and the slope are fine. Dropping rows costs power, not validity.
  • MAR: the mean of y is biased (we lost the high-x, high-y people). But the regression slope is fine. Because missingness depends only on x, and we’re modelling y given x, selection on the predictor doesn’t distort the conditional relationship. Whether complete-case analysis is “biased” depends on the question you ask, not just the mechanism.
  • MNAR: both are biased, including the slope, which is attenuated because we preferentially lost large residuals.

Multiple imputation: powerful, but not magic

Multiple imputation fills in each missing value several times with plausible draws from a model fit to the observed data, analyses each completed dataset, and pools the results with Rubin’s rules. Let’s estimate the mean of y under each mechanism.

mi_mean <- function(d, m = 20) {
  imp <- mice(d, m = m, method = "norm", printFlag = FALSE, seed = 1)
  est <- summary(pool(with(imp, lm(y ~ 1))))
  est$estimate
}

mi <- sapply(dat, mi_mean)
round(rbind(
  Truth          = rep(truth["mean_y"], 3),
  `Complete case` = cc[, "mean_y"],
  `Multiple imputation` = mi
), 3)
##                     mean_y mean_y mean_y
## Truth                2.003  2.003  2.003
## Complete case        2.005  1.379  1.293
## Multiple imputation  1.995  1.980  1.728

Under MAR, imputation recovers the truth: it uses x to predict what the missing y values probably were, and since x fully explains the selection, the predictions are right on average.

Under MNAR, imputation gets us partway but stops short. It helps because x is correlated with y, so using x recovers some of the lost high values. But it’s working exactly as designed, assuming the missing people look like observed people with the same x, and that assumption is false here. The imputation model can’t know that the missing values are systematically higher even among people with the same x, because nothing in the observed data says so.

The untestable heart of the problem

This brings us to the philosophically uncomfortable part.

You cannot distinguish MAR from MNAR using the data you have. The information that would tell you whether the missing values differ from the observed ones is precisely the information that’s missing. Molenberghs and colleagues (2008) made this precise: for every MNAR model, there is an MAR model that fits the observed data exactly as well. The two models agree about everything you can see and disagree only about what you can’t.

This has a few consequences worth sitting with:

  • Tests for “random” missingness only go so far. Little’s MCAR test, or comparing completers to non-completers on baseline variables, can provide evidence against MCAR. Nothing can provide evidence for MAR over MNAR. A non-significant test is not a certificate.
  • MAR is a substantive claim, not a statistical one. Defending it means arguing, from knowledge of how the data were collected, that you measured the things that drive missingness. That argument belongs in your methods section in plain words, not hidden behind “data were assumed MAR.”
  • The mechanism is a property of the variables you include. The same dataset can be MNAR given one set of covariates and (approximately) MAR given a richer set. Collecting “auxiliary” variables that predict dropout, such as reasons for leaving, engagement measures, or a final short-form outcome, is often the single most effective thing you can do about missing data, and it happens at the design stage, not the analysis stage.

There’s a Humean flavour here. We’re always inferring the unobserved from the observed, and that inference rests on an assumption of uniformity the observed data can’t justify on their own. MAR is the specific uniformity assumption missing-data analysis needs: that within strata of what we’ve seen, the unseen resembles the seen.

If you can’t test it, vary it: sensitivity analysis

If the key assumption is untestable, the honest response is not to pick one and pretend, but to show how much your conclusion depends on it. A simple and widely used approach is delta adjustment: impute under MAR, then shift the imputed values by some amount \(\delta\), representing the hypothesis that missing people differ systematically from observed people with the same covariates.

mice makes this easy through its post-processing hook:

delta_mean <- function(d, delta, m = 20) {
  ini  <- mice(d, maxit = 0, printFlag = FALSE)
  post <- ini$post
  post["y"] <- paste("imp[[j]][, i] <- imp[[j]][, i] +", delta)
  imp <- mice(d, m = m, method = "norm", post = post,
              printFlag = FALSE, seed = 1)
  summary(pool(with(imp, lm(y ~ 1))))$estimate
}

deltas <- seq(0, 1.5, by = 0.25)
sens <- data.frame(
  delta = deltas,
  estimate = sapply(deltas, function(dl) delta_mean(dat$MNAR, dl))
)

ggplot(sens, aes(delta, estimate)) +
  geom_line(colour = "#2C7FB8", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_point(colour = "#2C7FB8", size = 2.5) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = truth["mean_y"], linetype = "dashed") +
  annotate("text", x = 0.05, y = truth["mean_y"] + 0.04,
           label = "true mean", hjust = 0, size = 3.5) +
  labs(x = expression(delta ~ "(how much higher are the missing values?)"),
       y = "Estimated mean of y")

In real life, you don’t get the dashed line. What you get is the rest of the plot, which lets you say something like: “Our estimate holds up unless non-responders score more than about 0.5 points higher than comparable responders, which would be [plausible / implausible] because …” That sentence is far more informative than a single point estimate with an unstated assumption underneath it. It turns a hidden assumption into a visible, debatable one.

Choosing a plausible range for \(\delta\) is, again, not a statistical question. It’s a question for subject-matter knowledge, previous studies, or follow-up of a sample of non-responders.

Beyond Rubin: a causal view

Rubin’s framework is defined in terms of conditional probabilities, which makes it precise but sometimes hard to apply when you have many variables with missingness in several of them. A more recent line of work, associated with Karthika Mohan and Judea Pearl, represents missingness indicators as nodes in a causal graph (“m-graphs”). Instead of asking “is this MAR?” globally, you ask “given this causal story about why values go missing, is this particular quantity recoverable from the observed data?”

This sharpens a point our simulation already showed: whether missingness causes bias is not a blanket property of a dataset. It depends on the relationship between the missingness process and the specific estimand. Complete-case regression was fine for the slope under MAR-on-x and biased for the mean. Thinking causally about why data went missing helps you see which of your results are safe.

Practical takeaways

  • Treat NA as data about your data-collection process. Before choosing a method, write down a short story of why each variable might be missing.
  • Decide your estimand first. Distinguish values that exist but are unobserved from values that don’t exist at all.
  • Default methods carry default assumptions. Listwise deletion assumes (roughly) MCAR, or MAR on predictors for regression slopes. Multiple imputation and FIML assume MAR. Mean imputation is almost never a good idea, since it shrinks variances and distorts relationships under any mechanism.
  • Invest in auxiliary variables. Measuring what predicts missingness is what makes MAR plausible.
  • Never claim MAR was “verified.” Argue for it on substantive grounds, and report a sensitivity analysis showing what happens if you’re wrong.
  • Report missingness transparently. How much, in which variables, how it relates to observed characteristics, and what you did about it.

The empty cell is a reminder that our datasets are always partial views of the world, shaped by processes we didn’t fully control. Good missing-data practice isn’t about making the blanks disappear. It’s about being honest about what fills them.
